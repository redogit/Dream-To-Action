# First real-world pilot: one person gains one usable choice

**Status: proposed; not started.** The software prototype has been built and browser-checked. No real-world benefit has yet been demonstrated.

## The aim

Let one consenting person choose an important task they want to perform, identify one barrier, arrange the support or change needed, and observe whether the task becomes more usable. Do not require a particular income, lifestyle, ambition, or definition of success.

The person remains in charge of the goal and can revise, pause, or stop. Do not interpret participation, a setback, or a decision to stop as a measure of their worth. The recorded plan is not the person.

## A bounded first example

The included fictional example concerns independent completion of a work report. A form has unclear field labels and an unchecked keyboard path. A blank sample template and cooperation from its owner are possible dependencies, not resources already secured.

A useful result would be that the person can locate and complete the relevant field using their chosen access method and verify that the output is correct. Filling in this planner is not that result. Checking a fictional sample is not authorization to submit real work or make consequential decisions.

Another goal is equally valid. Start with what the person actually chooses, not the example supplied by the software.

## Run the first pass

1. **Choose and observe.** Ask the person what matters and what is currently difficult. Record one concrete task, the observed barrier, relevant conditions, and what improvement would count. Avoid collecting identity or background details not needed for that task. Separate direct observation from self-report and interpretation.
2. **Confirm the conditions.** Identify one small change. Separate the participant's action from the helper's action or an institutional change. Ask the actual helper whether they can provide it; mark support as unconfirmed until there is a real answer. Choose an acceptable time, effort, and resource limit with the participant. Do not turn a blocked step into a demand to try harder.
3. **Try and compare.** With permission and adequate conditions, try the changed task. Prefer changing one relevant barrier first so the observation is interpretable. Note differences in conditions; one attempt is not proof of a general causal effect. Stop or revise when there is harm, unacceptable burden, unreliable output, or withdrawal of consent.
4. **Review with the person.** Record what became possible, what still failed, any added costs, and the participant's assessment. Keep the earlier plan and the new observation separate. Count progress only when a meaningful barrier actually changed or a chosen action became usable. Agree on the next step rather than automatically expanding the project.

## What success is—and is not

The immediate software milestone is a usable, correctable local record. The real-world milestone is a participant-confirmed improvement in an important action, with enough observations to describe its scope and limits.

Neither a completed form, a favorable readiness message, a helper's name, nor an attractive dashboard proves that opportunity exists. A commitment is not delivery. Delivery of a resource is not automatically a beneficial outcome. A single helpful result is a bounded result, not proof of a national solution.

Before broader use, check the actual browser and access method, including screen readers where relevant. Rework the tool when it increases burden. Wider opportunity also requires real resources and institutional changes; those responsibilities must not disappear into an individual action plan.
